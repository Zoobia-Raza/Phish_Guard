03 / AVOID IT

Stay off the hook

Defenders don't need luck — they need a 6-second ritual before clicking anything. Six habits, in the order you'll actually use them. Tap each one for the full drill.

// THE PRE-CLICK RITUAL
  1. 01

    Read the real address

    Display names lie. The domain after the @ is the only truth.

    Attackers can set any display name — "NovaBank Security", "Your School IT", even a friend's name. Open the actual address and read the domain backwards: novabank-alerts.com is not novabank.com. Watch for extra words, hyphens, and swapped letters (rn pretending to be m).

  2. 02

    Hover before you click

    One second on a link reveals its real destination.

    On desktop, rest your cursor on any link and read the preview in the corner. On phone, press and hold. If the destination doesn't match the brand's true domain — don't click. Read domains right-to-left: the part just before the first single slash is what actually matters.

  3. 03

    Refuse to be rushed

    Urgency is the attack, not the alarm.

    "Within 24 hours", "act now", "final warning" — legitimate companies don't threaten you into clicking. If a message manufactures pressure, the pressure itself is the red flag. Slow down on purpose; attackers profit from your panic.

  4. 04

    Type, don't click

    Reach banking and school portals through your own fingers.

    Never sign in from a link in an email or text. Open a new tab and type the address yourself, or use the official app you installed. Thirty extra seconds beats a stolen account every single time.

  5. 05

    Verify on a second channel

    Confirm through a channel the attacker doesn't control.

    Got a scary "bank" email? Call the number printed on your card or on their official website — never the one in the email. For school, ask the teacher or the IT desk in person. A phisher controls one channel; they can't control two.

  6. 06

    Report it, then delete it

    One report protects a hundred people.

    Use your mail app's "Report phishing" button, or forward the mail to your IT team or teacher — then delete it. Don't just ignore it: the next name on the attacker's list might not know what you now know.
